Anatomy of a Bad WeldThe ten defects that give the welder away — every one of them has a precise cause, and almost always, an equally precise fix

Trapped slag, cracks, holes, humps. Every welding defect has a precise cause and, almost always, an equally precise fix. But a perfect weld bead starts long before the arc ever strikes — with the shape given to the edges being joined.

Look at a weld bead and most people see a row of metallic scales. An inspector sees something else entirely: the electrode's angle, the current that was used, whether the welder was rushing or working calmly. Every mistake leaves its own signature, and learning to read it is the first step toward not repeating it.

01

First, the bevel

Two thick plates butted edge to edge don't weld well — the electrode simply can't reach the bottom of the joint. That's why the edges get prepared with a bevel, chosen based on thickness, joint symmetry, and the need to use as little filler metal as possible.

Up to 6 mm, a square-edge joint is enough, leaving a root gap equal to a quarter or half the thickness. Beyond that, the joint moves to a V-preparation: 60° on plate, with a 2-3 mm root gap and roughly a 1.5 mm root face; 70° on pipe, with different rules for uphill and downhill welding. Above 20 mm, a U-preparation takes over; between 15 and 40 mm, an X (double-V) preparation, symmetric or not. With a backing strip on the far side, the root gap can go to zero and the first layer can run in two passes.

The golden rule, illustrated in every manual with a blunt "NO / YES": the bevel has to let the electrode actually reach in and fill it.

02

The ritual of striking the arc

With a coated electrode, even striking the arc is its own technique. The electrode gets scratched against the workpiece about 5 cm before the actual starting point to strike the arc, then pulled back immediately without touching down, carried at a certain speed to the real starting point, and only then advanced slowly. The gap between the electrode tip and the pool has to stay roughly equal to the electrode's own diameter.

03

Slag, undercut, and cracking

Slag inclusions show up at poorly executed restarts and in multi-pass welds — the fault of poor technique, too large an electrode, or slag that wasn't cleaned off properly. Undercut shows up as a groove eaten into the base metal along the edges of the bead, common on flat-position fillet welds: the fix is lowering a current that was set too high, or shortening an arc that was running too long.

Cracking — hot or cold — is among the most serious defects a welded structure can have. A longitudinal crack in the fusion zone usually traces back to the wrong electrode: the fix is depositing fuller beads, moving more slowly, and not weaving. A crack running alongside the bead is typical of alloy steels and cast iron: here the fix is preheating the base metal, switching electrodes, laying down thinner layers, and moving faster.

04

Lack of penetration and a wandering bead

Lack of penetration on a flat joint shows up as unfused metal right at the root of the bevel: the fix is opening the bevel wider at the root, or switching to a thinner electrode, and raising the current. On a corner or fillet joint, the same defect eats into the load-bearing cross-section — again, more current is the fix. An irregular bead shape gives away an inexperienced welder: a larger electrode, ideally rutile, and a calmer hand fix it.

05

Blowholes, underfill, overfill, and porosity

Blowholes tend to form in the first 2-3 cm of a weld, mostly when the electrodes are damp, the workpiece is dirty, or the material contains elements that make it hard to weld at all — as in so-called leaded steels. Underfill simply means not enough filler was deposited: the fix is slowing down, choosing a larger electrode diameter, and tilting the electrode a bit more. Overfill (excess reinforcement) is the opposite problem — speed up, weave slightly, and adjust the electrode angle. And fine porosity itself comes from grease, paint, or rust in the pool, or from an arc that's been held too long.

06

Choosing the right electrode

Many of these defects get avoided further upstream, in electrode selection. Choosing the right electrode means accounting for the base metal's chemical composition, its tensile and toughness properties, its metallurgical structure, and the welding position. European standards help here: EN 499 classifies coated electrodes with an alphanumeric designation, while EN 757, EN 1599, and EN 1600 cover high-strength steels, creep-resistant steels, and stainless / heat-resistant steels respectively. The standard's tables assign every code number a minimum tensile and yield strength for the deposited metal — ranging from 430-460 N/mm² for type 60 up to 830 N/mm² for type 120.
