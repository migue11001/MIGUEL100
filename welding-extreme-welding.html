From a 3,100° Flame to a Sea of PowderExtreme welding: oxy-acetylene, submerged arc, and narrow-gap — three techniques for when there's too much metal, or it sits in the wrong place

The oxy-acetylene torch is the oldest of the modern welding systems, and it still burns in workshops today. Submerged arc welding works hidden beneath a layer of granular flux, at currents up to 700 amps. Narrow-gap welding joins plates 30 centimeters thick. Three techniques for when there's a lot of metal to weld — or it sits somewhere hard to reach.

Not every weld gets made on a bench with a torch in hand. There are pipes in impossible positions, ship hulls, pressure vessels with walls as thick as a human arm. For each of these cases, engineering has invented its own way of melting metal.

01

The torch: open-air chemistry

Oxy-acetylene welding is an autogenous flame process: a mixture of acetylene and oxygen reaching roughly 3,100 °C. It's simple, the equipment is compact, and it suits awkward pipe positions and root passes especially well. But the heat spreads over a wide area, the heat-affected zones run large, and shrinkage is significant — which is why it's limited to thin material, plate between 6 and 8 mm.

02

Anatomy of a flame

The flame itself is a tiny three-stage chemical reactor. Right at the nozzle, primary combustion happens: acetylene reacts with the oxygen fed from the cylinder, forming carbon monoxide and hydrogen and releasing about a third of the total heat in a bright cone called the inner cone, at roughly 1,200 °C. Immediately after, that carbon monoxide and hydrogen burn with oxygen drawn from the surrounding air, forming carbon dioxide and water — the reducing zone, responsible for the other two-thirds of the heat, where temperatures peak at 3,100 °C. Further out, the gases stay incandescent in the outer plume, at around 2,400 °C.

Get the oxygen ratio right and the flame is neutral. Run it lean and carbon migrates into the pool — a carburizing flame. Run it rich and the oxidizing flame gives up oxygen to the molten metal instead.

Lighting and shutting down the torch both follow a strict sequence — acetylene first, then oxygen, to light it; acetylene off first, then oxygen, then the cylinder valves, to shut it down — and that order has to be followed to the letter to avoid flashbacks into the cylinders and explosions.

03

Forehand or backhand

Even the travel direction matters. Working right-to-left, forehand, with a weaving motion, welds thin square-edge plate well — but the flame plays over a wider area and the grain coarsens. Working left-to-right, backhand, with the flame aimed straight at the pool and a small circular motion that stirs up a kind of vortex, gives less dilution and deeper penetration — the method of choice on thicker plate.

04

Submerged arc: welding in the dark

In submerged arc welding (SAW), the arc strikes between the workpiece and a continuous wire, but nobody actually sees it — it's buried under a granular flux fed from a hopper, which protects the pool from oxidation, can add alloying elements and deoxidizers, and forms a slag layer that has to be removed afterward. The process runs fully or partially automated and produces high-quality joints, but it can't be used out of position — the flux would simply fall away.

The numbers here belong to a different league entirely. Where MIG/MAG runs 180–200 amps, SAW runs up to 700 amps, at 40–45 volts; push beyond that and the pool grows too large, exposes the arc, and invites porosity. Wire diameters run from 1.6 to 6 mm, copper- or nickel-coated, and multiple wires can run together. The minimum thickness is 8 mm — below that, the current simply burns straight through the joint.

The operator stays safe because the arc is buried, but the high heat input invites hot cracking, and flux that hasn't been properly dried is hygroscopic enough to introduce hydrogen and cold cracking. Process stability comes down to one simple relationship: melting rate has to equal wire feed rate. With wire this thick, the machine struggles to hold that balance on its own — which is why systems exist that tie the wire-feed motor directly to arc voltage.

05

Two families of flux

Flux comes in two families. Fused fluxes are glassy, made by melting minerals at 1,500–1,700 °C and grinding the result: chemically inert, non-hygroscopic, reusable, and well suited to high travel speeds. Bonded (agglomerated) fluxes are baked at lower temperatures with a binder, which lets them keep an active metallurgical role and add manganese and silicon to the weld — but they're hygroscopic and can't be reused. Mixed fluxes try to land somewhere between the two.

06

Narrow-gap: the tight cut

For plate from 25 to 300 mm thick, there's narrow-gap welding: the edges are prepared with parallel faces or a shallow U, filling a dramatically smaller weld volume than a conventional bevel would need. The gains show up across the board — less energy, less filler metal, less time, and less distortion in the finished part. The U-shape is generally preferred, since fusing the walls at the bottom of the joint is easier and shrinkage is less of a problem. The torches themselves are built for precise mechanical control, and the technique applies to MIG/MAG, TIG (high quality, low productivity, for low-alloy and stainless steels), and submerged arc alike.

Acetylene isn't the hottest-burning gas by accident. Fuel gas tables show propane topping out at 2,526 °C and methane at 2,538 °C, while acetylene reaches 3,087 °C using just 2.5 volumes of oxygen per volume of gas. It's that ratio, not just the raw temperature, that makes it irreplaceable at the torch.
