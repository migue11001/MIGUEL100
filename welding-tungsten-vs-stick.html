Tungsten vs. Stick: The Two Faces of the Electric ArcTIG is slow, demanding, and expensive — but it produces flawless beads. The coated electrode is the most widely used welding process on Earth

TIG is slow, demanding, and expensive, but it produces flawless beads. The coated electrode process is the most widely used welding method on Earth — it works almost anywhere, on almost anything. Behind both lies the exact same phenomenon: an electric arc, tamed in two completely opposite ways.

01

One arc, two philosophies

An electric arc is a discharge that jumps between two conductors through an ionized gas, reaching temperatures capable of melting any metal on the planet. The real challenge isn't striking that arc — it's controlling it.

Two techniques do that in radically different ways: one uses an electrode that never wears away, the other one built to consume itself on purpose.

02

TIG: the electrode that never melts

In TIG (Tungsten Inert Gas), the arc strikes between the workpiece and a non-consumable tungsten electrode — a metal with an extremely high melting point and excellent thermionic emission properties — all shielded by an inert gas. Electrodes come in four types: pure tungsten, thoriated (with thorium oxide), zirconiated, or striped thoriated tungsten. The process can run manually, semi-automatically (the machine controls arc and electrode, the welder just guides it along), or fully automatically, handed off to a robot.

The arc is stable and gives excellent control over the weld pool, but TIG isn't very productive — it's reserved mostly for thinner material. In exchange, it welds virtually every metal, with a filler rod or even without one, simply fusing the two edges together with the arc's extremely concentrated energy.

03

A matter of polarity

TIG runs on either direct or alternating current, and that choice changes everything. With direct current, electrode negative (DCEN), the positive terminal sits on the workpiece: electrons bombard the base metal at extremely high velocity, producing a deep, narrow weld pool while the electrode itself stays relatively cool. With electrode positive (DCEP), the torch carries the positive charge and heat concentrates on the electrode instead — that risks damaging the tungsten, so it's almost never used, except at low currents when the goal is breaking up surface oxides: the heavy positive ions slam into the workpiece and shatter them.

Alternating current splits the heat 50/50 between electrode and workpiece, putting that same "thermionic sandblasting" effect to work stripping away the stubborn oxide layer on aluminum and magnesium.

04

Keeping the pool clean

TIG produces no slag at all, so the joint edges have to be scrupulously clean beforehand to avoid hot cracking, and the electrode must never touch the pool at arc strike or it will contaminate it with tungsten. On sensitive materials like titanium, a trailing shield (a "slipper") keeps blowing gas over the bead as it cools; on stainless tube, an inert atmosphere gets purged inside the pipe itself. The typical defects here are tungsten inclusions, root-side oxidation, gas porosity, and contamination of the shielding gas.

05

Coated electrode: the smart stick

The most widely used process in the workshop and on the structural steel floor is coated (stick) electrode welding, suited to nearly every ferrous and non-ferrous alloy except aluminum. Here the electrode is consumable: a metal core acts as the filler metal, wrapped in a coating. When the arc strikes, the core melts into droplets that fall into the pool; the coating melts right along with it, forming a small cup at the electrode's tip that builds up a local gas overpressure strong enough to detach each droplet, while part of it vaporizes into a protective atmosphere.

Once in the pool, the coating reacts chemically — thanks to calcium carbonate — deoxidizing, dephosphorizing, and desulfurizing the weld metal, then leaves behind a solid slag layer that shields it while it solidifies.

06

The equipment behind the stick

The equipment itself is simple: a power source and two leads — one for the ground clamp, one for the electrode holder. The power source needs a drooping output characteristic, so the natural variations in arc length — unavoidable with a human hand holding the electrode — don't send the current swinging wildly. Modern inverter power sources rectify the incoming mains current, modulate it electronically at frequencies up to 100 kHz, run it through a tiny transformer, and rectify it again — the result is equipment that's both lightweight and reliable.

07

Acid, rutile, cellulosic, or basic?

The coating decides an electrode's whole personality. Acid coatings, based on iron, manganese, and silicon oxides, give a stable arc and a fluid pool, but aren't suited to positional welding. Rutile coatings, built around titanium dioxide, give the best-looking bead and are the most widely used for moderate thicknesses — but they don't chemically refine the pool and they release hydrogen. Cellulosic coatings gasify almost their entire mass, penetrate deeply, and allow vertical-down welding, but do little against impurities.

Basic (low-hydrogen) coatings, rich in calcium and magnesium carbonates plus fluorite, actively scrub sulfur and phosphorus out of the pool and are the electrode of choice for materials prone to hot cracking.

The trade-off: fluorite destabilizes the arc, so basic electrodes need DC with electrode positive, and the coating is so hygroscopic it has to be oven-dried before use.

An electrode's code is its ID card.
