Punches and Dies: The Duel Fought in Hundredths of a MillimeterThey're the die's "working" parts — one pushes, the other receives. Between them sits a razor-thin gap that decides both part quality and tool life

A progressive die's plates are its skeleton. Its punches and dies are its teeth. Between the two sits a space measured in hundredths of a millimeter, and when that space finally wears too wide to hold, the tooling isn't thrown away — it gets "reloaded" with shims as thin as a human hair.

01

Punch meets die

If the plates are the die's skeleton, the punch and the die (the female cavity) are its teeth. The punch is the element that comes down and cuts, bends, or coins the sheet metal; the die is the fixed counterpart that receives it. Their relationship comes down to microscopic distances — and a large part of a tool designer's job is deciding exactly how close those two surfaces should sit.

02

A head for every punch

A punch has to be anchored to the top plate through the punch plate, and how that's actually done is far from trivial. The most common approach is the bolted head: the punch is fixed with one or more screws to a head machined from hardened K720 tool steel, which absorbs the stripping force on the way back up. It's straightforward to build and it shields the backing plate from wear.

For off-the-shelf round punches, a supplementary head is often used instead — a simple ring whose only job is lifting the punch, and which doesn't even need to be hardened. When a punch is too small to thread, the brazed head takes over: the punch is embedded in an iron head with a 0.10 mm radial clearance, filled by the brazing alloy. It's treated as a critical solution, used only when nothing else works.

There's also the dowelled punch, pinned internally or externally, reserved almost exclusively for bending and coining punches; the collet head, wire-EDM'd, which grips the punch like a clothespin — convenient, but unreliable on thicker strip; and finally the punch screwed straight into the top plate, typical of doweled dies where the punch plate itself carries no clearance at all.

03

The secret to long life: shims

Every time a blanking punch wears, the fix is to regrind it — removing the worn edge. But that shortens the punch, and in a progressive die, every tenth of a millimeter matters. The solution is as simple as it is clever: hardened shims (commonly called Hasberg shims), wire-cut to the exact same profile as the punch, get stacked between the head and the punch to make up the difference.

A full shim set spans 0.10, 0.20, 0.30, 0.40, 0.50, 1.00, 1.50, and 2.00 mm, and stacking them reaches any dimension in between — to recover 1.70 mm, for instance, a toolmaker stacks 1.00 + 0.50 + 0.20.

The full set adds up to 6 mm. Once that's used up, the punch has reached the end of its life and gets replaced.

Punches with a supplementary, brazed, or collet head need two full sets — one above the head and one below — swapped between each other at every regrind so the punch's overall length never changes. Dowelled punches can't use shims at the head at all; the only option there is shimming the punch plate itself, which is why that plate is designed in several separate sections specifically to allow it.

04

The angle that saves the edge

The hardest-working punch in the die is the blanking punch, because cutting without a burr demands a perfect edge every single stroke. To slow that wear down, the cutting face is angled — the shear angle (cutting relief). Instead of striking the sheet with its whole face at once, the punch engages the material progressively as the die descends, spreading a single force spike into many smaller ones.

05

The die and the clearance that decides everything

On the other side of the strip sits the die — almost always an insert housed in the die plate. The parameter that governs everything here is the cutting clearance: the gap between the punch profile and the die profile. Too large, and the cut takes little force but leaves a visible burr. Too small, and the burr disappears but the force climbs, wearing out both punch and die sooner. There's no universal formula — every shop keeps its own empirical tables linking clearance to the sheet's material and thickness.

An often underrated ally here is oil: wetting the strip's surface before it enters the die cuts down both burr and cutting force, and extends the life of every cutting edge in the tool.

The die's internal geometry matters just as much. Below the cutting edge, where the relief angle begins, a scrap-relief opening lets the cut slug clear the tool — and the three most common combinations are a cylindrical relief with a cylindrical clearance, a cylindrical relief with a conical clearance, or both conical. Dies get shimmed with Hasberg shims too, with one caveat: the shim profile has to be oversized by roughly 0.35 mm radially relative to the punch, or the scrap won't drop through cleanly.
