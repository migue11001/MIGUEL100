Materials Physics · Module Welding

The Wire That Never Runs OutHow MIG/MAG welding works — a continuous wire, an electric arc, and a cloud of gas protecting the molten metal

It's the most widely used process in automated fabrication: a steel wire feeding without pause, an electric arc, and a shielding gas cloud protecting the molten metal. But before welding anything, you first have to understand what actually happens to metal when it's heated — and that's where a formula comes in.

01

Weldable, or not?

Take two pieces of steel, heat them until they melt together at one point, and let them cool — they've joined. It sounds simple, but that melted spot is never a purely local event. Heat spreads outward, changes the metal's structure for a few millimeters around it, and it's precisely there — not in the melted zone itself — that the most dangerous defects tend to hide.

A material is called weldable when it can form a joint with genuine metallic continuity. Every welded joint splits into two regions: the fusion zone (FZ), where the metal actually turned liquid and then resolidified, and the heat-affected zone (HAZ) — metal that never melted, but still went through a full heating-and-cooling cycle.

The HAZ is the one under special watch: hot cracks, cold cracks, and lamellar tearing tend to show up there, not in the fusion zone itself.

To keep it in check on low-alloy steels, welders rely on the impact (notch-toughness) test, which measures the joint's metallic continuity directly.

02

The heat formula

How much energy is actually going into the metal? Welders calculate it as the specific heat input, factor Q:

// Heat Input
Q = (I × V × 60) / v, expressed in joules per centimeter, where I is current in amperes, V is voltage, and v is travel speed in centimeters per minute. More current and voltage means more heat; the faster the travel speed, the less heat lands per centimeter of weld.

But Q isn't the whole story. Part thickness matters too, along with preheat temperature — typically 50 to 300 °C — which slows the cooling rate: cool the metal too fast, and cracks form in both the fusion zone and the HAZ. Another key parameter is the dilution ratio Rd: the percentage of melted base metal within the total volume of the fusion zone. It shifts from one process to another, and it tells you how much the filler metal actually mixed in with the parent material.

03

MIG or MAG? It depends on the gas

The continuous-wire process is industry's workhorse, thanks to its productivity and how readily it automates. The arc strikes between the workpiece and a consumable wire that a feed unit pushes toward the weld pool without ever stopping — unlike a stick electrode, which has to be replaced once it's consumed, the wire simply never runs out. A shielding gas, blown straight onto the molten pool, protects it from the atmosphere.

The name depends entirely on that gas. It's MIG (Metal Inert Gas) when the shielding gas is inert — typically argon — and MAG (Metal Active Gas) when it's active, like CO₂, or an argon-CO₂ blend even with only a small active fraction. Pure argon covers aluminum, copper, and its alloys like bronze; an argon-CO₂ blend, in various ratios, covers everything else — plain steel and stainless included.

04

Four ways to drop a bead

The wire's metal reaches the pool as droplets, and exactly how they detach depends on the voltage and current set on the power source. In spray transfer, a continuous stream of small, free-flying droplets forms — it needs high current, but the arc stays stable and the bead looks clean. In globular transfer, at medium currents, the droplets grow larger and detach at a lower frequency, pinched off by the magnetic field like a pair of scissors closing — the bead here doesn't look nearly as good. In short-circuit transfer, at low currents, the large droplets detach through an actual short circuit; it's the mode used for positional welding and for fill passes.

There's a fourth path too — pulsed arc — where the power source pulses the current instead of holding it steady, letting the right equipment weld almost any material while all but eliminating spatter.

05

Strengths and weaknesses

MIG/MAG welds nearly every ferrous material and plenty of non-ferrous ones too, delivers high quality, runs in every position, leaves no slag behind, and is both highly productive and easy to learn. The trade-offs: the equipment is bulky, the pool is less protected than in stick or submerged-arc welding, the process is sensitive to drafts, and shielding gas isn't free.

In MIG/MAG, the exact same machine can produce completely different beads just by changing voltage and wire feed speed. With the same 2.4 mm wire, going from 300 to 650 amps turns a narrow, tall bead into a wide, deeply penetrating one. That's why the parameter "recipe" is the real secret every welder guards.
